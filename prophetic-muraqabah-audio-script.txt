TITLE: MURAQABAH IN THE LIFE OF PROPHET MUHAMMAD ﷺ
SUBTITLE: BEFORE AND AFTER PROPHETHOOD
ESTIMATED LENGTH: 15–20 MINUTES

[Opening]

Bismillāhir-Raḥmānir-Raḥīm.

Al-ḥamdu lillāhi Rabbil-ʿālamīn. We praise Allah, we seek His help, and we ask Him for forgiveness. Peace and blessings be upon our master Muhammad ﷺ, upon his family, his Companions, and all those who follow his guidance until the Last Day.

Today we are reflecting on a beautiful subject: muraqabah in the life of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, before and after prophethood.

Muraqabah means spiritual watchfulness. It means that a person lives with the awareness that Allah sees him, hears him, knows his thoughts, knows his intentions, and is fully aware of his public and private life.

The word muraqabah is a later scholarly term, but its meaning is firmly established in the Qur’an and Sunnah. The Prophet ﷺ described its highest level in the famous Hadith of Jibrīl:

“Ihsan is that you worship Allah as though you see Him; and if you cannot see Him, then know that He sees you.”

This hadith is recorded in Sahih Muslim, Hadith 8.

There are two levels in this teaching. The first is to worship Allah as though you see Him. This means worshipping with presence, reverence, love, awe, and sincerity. The second is that, even if we cannot reach that level, we must know with certainty that Allah sees us.

This knowledge changes a person. It changes what he says when he is alone. It changes what he watches when nobody is present. It changes how he earns money, how he treats his family, how he responds when angry, and how he behaves when no one can praise or criticize him.

To understand this quality, we should look at the life of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ.

[Before prophethood: seclusion in Hira]

Before the first revelation, the Prophet ﷺ lived in Makkah among a society deeply affected by idolatry, injustice, tribal arrogance, exploitation of the weak, and moral corruption.

Yet Muhammad ﷺ was different from his people. He was known for truthfulness and trustworthiness. He did not participate in their idol worship. He was not attracted to their corruption and excess.

As he approached the age of forty, he developed a love for solitude.

ʿĀ’ishah رضي الله عنها narrates in Sahih al-Bukhari, Hadith 3:

“The commencement of the Divine Inspiration to Allah’s Messenger ﷺ was in the form of true dreams... Then the love of seclusion was bestowed upon him. He used to go in seclusion in the Cave of Hira, where he used to worship Allah alone continuously for several nights before returning to his family.”

He would take provisions with him, remain in the cave for a period of time, and then return to Khadījah رضي الله عنها to collect more provisions before going back again.

The Arabic word used in this narration is taḥannuth. The scholars explain it as worship, devotion, and turning away from the religion and practices of the people of Quraysh.

We should be precise here. The hadith does not give us a detailed description of a formal meditation method. It does not tell us that the Prophet ﷺ followed a later spiritual exercise with a prescribed posture, breathing pattern, or repeated visualization.

What it clearly establishes is that before prophethood he withdrew from society, worshipped Allah alone, reflected deeply, and kept away from idolatry.

This was not an escape from responsibility. It was preparation for the greatest responsibility ever given to a human being.

The Messenger of Allah ﷺ was about to receive revelation. He was about to carry the Qur’an, confront falsehood, teach humanity, establish justice, and guide people to Allah. His heart was prepared through truthfulness, purity, worship, and seclusion.

Then, in the Cave of Hira, the truth came to him.

The angel Jibrīl عليه السلام appeared and commanded him: “Read.”

The Prophet ﷺ replied that he could not read. The angel repeated the command, and then the opening verses of Surah al-ʿAlaq were revealed:

“Read in the name of your Lord who created.
He created humanity from a clinging substance.
Read, and your Lord is the Most Generous—
Who taught by the pen—
Taught humanity what it did not know.”

These are the first revealed verses of the Qur’an, recorded in Surah al-ʿAlaq, 96:1–5.

Before revelation, the Prophet ﷺ was not seeking fame, authority, or worldly power. He was in a state of devotion when Allah chose him for the prophetic mission.

This teaches us that spiritual preparation comes before public influence. Before Allah gives a person a responsibility that affects others, He may first develop that person inwardly through sincerity, hardship, reflection, worship, and a deep awareness of Allah.

[Was this modern meditation?]

When people today use the word meditation, they may mean different things. Some mean relaxation. Some mean breathing exercises. Some mean emptying the mind. Others mean reflection upon the purpose of life.

We should therefore be careful when speaking about the Prophet ﷺ.

There is no authentic report that the Prophet ﷺ taught a modern, standardized meditation system involving a particular posture, breathing technique, visualization exercise, or the emptying of the mind.

However, authentic reports clearly show that the Prophet ﷺ practiced deep reflection, remembrance, recitation, night prayer, and devotional seclusion.

The Islamic terms are more precise:

Tafakkur means reflection and contemplation.

Tadabbur means pondering the meanings and consequences of revelation.

Dhikr means remembrance of Allah.

Tahajjud means rising at night for prayer.

Iʿtikāf means devotional retreat in the mosque.

Taḥannuth describes the worship and devotional seclusion of the Prophet ﷺ in Hira before revelation.

So, if by meditation we mean emptying the mind or following a non-Islamic religious technique, we should not attribute that to the Prophet ﷺ without evidence.

But if by meditation we mean sincere reflection upon Allah, His creation, His revelation, death, accountability, and the purpose of life, then the life of the Prophet ﷺ contains clear examples of this kind of spiritual contemplation.

[After prophethood: muraqabah in daily life]

After prophethood, the Prophet ﷺ did not remain permanently in a cave. His life became one of constant engagement.

He taught people, led the community, visited the sick, attended funerals, resolved disputes, cared for his family, dealt with opponents, and established justice.

His muraqabah continued—not as permanent isolation, but as constant awareness of Allah while fulfilling every responsibility.

This is a crucial distinction. Islam does not teach us to abandon the world. It teaches us to live in the world without allowing the world to take possession of the heart.

A person may be alone in a cave and still be distracted. Another person may be in a busy marketplace, surrounded by people, and yet his heart may be deeply aware of Allah.

The real question is not only, “Where am I?” The real question is, “Where is my heart?”

[The Prophet’s ﷺ contemplation of the heavens]

Allah says in Surah Āl ʿImrān:

“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are signs for people of understanding—those who remember Allah while standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth, saying: Our Lord, You have not created this without purpose.”

These are Qur’an 3:190–191.

These verses join two actions together. First is dhikr: the heart remembers Allah. Second is tafakkur: the mind reflects upon Allah’s creation.

The believer looks at the sky, the alternation of night and day, the order of creation, the weakness of the human being, and the temporary nature of this world. But his reflection does not stop at created things. It leads him to the Creator.

The purpose is not simply to admire the universe. The purpose is to recognize Allah’s power, wisdom, mercy, and greatness.

Ibn ʿAbbās رضي الله عنهما narrated that he spent a night in the house of his aunt Maymūnah رضي الله عنها. During the night, the Prophet ﷺ awoke, looked toward the sky, and recited the verses:

“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are signs for people of understanding...”

He then performed wuḍū’ and stood for prayer.

This report is recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari, Hadith 183, with related narrations in Sahih Muslim, Hadith 763.

This was not meditation in the sense of disconnecting from worship or emptying the mind. It was a complete spiritual movement:

The eyes looked at the sky.

The tongue recited the Qur’an.

The heart reflected upon Allah.

The body stood in prayer.

The soul made supplication.

This is the Prophetic model of contemplation: reflection that leads to remembrance, remembrance that leads to prayer, and prayer that leads to transformation.

[Night prayer and gratitude]

The night was a special time for the Prophet ﷺ.

He would stand before Allah when people were asleep. His night prayer was not merely a physical exercise. It was a private meeting with his Lord.

The Prophet ﷺ would recite slowly, reflect upon the meanings of the Qur’an, bow, prostrate, and supplicate.

In an authentic narration, the Prophet ﷺ stood in prayer until his feet became swollen. When he was asked why he did this despite Allah’s forgiveness, he said:

“Should I not be a grateful servant?”

This is recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari, Hadith 1130, and Sahih Muslim, Hadith 2820.

His night worship shows us that Prophetic contemplation was not passive. It strengthened his relationship with Allah and gave him strength for the work of the day.

The night nourished the heart; the day was spent serving the creation.

[Dhikr at every moment]

ʿĀ’ishah رضي الله عنها said:

“The Messenger of Allah ﷺ used to remember Allah at all times.”

This is recorded in Sahih Muslim, Hadith 373.

This means that remembrance was not restricted to one place, one sound, or one posture.

He remembered Allah at home and in the mosque. He remembered Allah while traveling and while dealing with people. He remembered Allah during ease and hardship.

His spiritual awareness continued while he taught, judged, ate, rested, and cared for his family.

This gives us an important lesson: the goal is not to create a short spiritual experience that disappears when the session ends. The goal is to carry awareness of Allah into ordinary life.

A Muslim may spend ten quiet minutes in reflection, but the effect of those ten minutes should be seen in the next conversation, the next transaction, the next prayer, and the next private decision.

[Iʿtikāf after prophethood]

Before prophethood, the Prophet ﷺ retreated to Hira.

After prophethood, the established form of spiritual retreat that he regularly practiced was iʿtikāf, especially in the mosque during the last ten nights of Ramadan.

ʿĀ’ishah رضي الله عنها said:

“The Prophet ﷺ used to observe iʿtikāf during the last ten days of Ramadan until Allah took him, and his wives continued iʿtikāf after him.”

This is recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari, Hadith 2026.

This shows the difference between the historical retreat of Hira before revelation and the legislated practice of iʿtikāf after revelation.

Hira has historical importance because the first revelation began there. But the Sunnah after prophethood directed Muslims toward prayer, remembrance, Qur’an, iʿtikāf, charity, service, and obedience.

The location alone does not create holiness in a person. It is sincerity and obedience to Allah that matter.

[The early Muslims and muraqabah]

The early generations understood muraqabah as something practical.

Hasan al-Basrī رحمه الله, one of the great early scholars and worshippers, said:

“The believer is responsible for himself. He holds himself to account for the sake of Allah.”

This statement is reported in early books of asceticism, including the Musannaf of Ibn Abī Shaybah.

Hasan al-Basrī explained that the reckoning will be lighter for people who held themselves accountable in this world, while those who lived without self-examination will face a more difficult reckoning in the Hereafter.

This is the relationship between muraqabah and muḥāsabah.

Muraqabah means knowing that Allah sees you.

Muḥāsabah means examining yourself because Allah sees you.

One early statement attributed to ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb رضي الله عنه says:

“Hold yourselves accountable before you are held accountable, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed.”

This saying is transmitted in early works such as Kitāb al-Zuhd and the Musannaf of Ibn Abī Shaybah. It should be presented as a well-known saying attributed to ʿUmar, not automatically as a hadith of the Prophet ﷺ.

Its meaning is supported by the Qur’an. Allah says in Surah al-Hashr, 59:18:

“O believers, be mindful of Allah, and let every soul look to what it has sent forth for tomorrow.”

This is muraqabah in daily life.

Before going to sleep, ask yourself:

What did I say today?

Whom did I hurt?

Was my prayer performed with attention?

Did I seek Allah’s pleasure or people’s praise?

Did I guard my eyes and my tongue?

Did I fulfill the rights of my family?

Did I repent when I made a mistake?

This is not obsessive self-hatred. It is honest spiritual maintenance.

A believer does not examine himself in order to despair. He examines himself in order to return to Allah.

[Muraqabah in private]

One of the clearest signs of muraqabah is what a person does when no human being is watching.

The Prophet ﷺ mentioned among the people who will receive Allah’s special shade on the Day when there will be no shade except His shade:

“A man who remembers Allah in solitude and his eyes overflow with tears.”

This is recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari, Hadith 6479, and other authentic narrations.

Why is private worship so valuable?

Because when a person remembers Allah alone, with no audience and no praise, the heart is closer to sincerity.

This does not mean that public worship is always insincere. Rather, it teaches us to develop deeds that nobody knows about except Allah.

A private prayer.

A hidden charity.

A sin abandoned for the sake of Allah.

A tear shed from fear and hope.

An apology offered without being forced.

A temptation rejected even though nobody would have known.

These are moments of muraqabah.

[How to practice Prophetic contemplation today]

From the example of the Prophet ﷺ, we can develop a simple practice without claiming that it is a specific Sunnah ritual.

Choose a quiet time, preferably before Fajr or after ʿIshā’.

Begin with praise of Allah and sincere repentance.

Recite a small portion of the Qur’an in a language you understand.

Reflect on one meaning. For example:

“Allah sees me.”

“What blessing has Allah given me today?”

“What sin do I need to leave?”

“What responsibility will Allah ask me about?”

Then make duʿā’.

After that, pray two voluntary units if possible, or perform another established act of worship.

Finally, take one practical step. Repair a relationship. Return something that does not belong to you. Guard your tongue. Give charity. Pray on time. Leave a sin.

This final step is important. Prophetic reflection was always connected to action.

[Conclusion]

So, did the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ meditate?

If by meditation we mean a modern, standardized technique with a prescribed posture and breathing method, we should not attribute that to the Prophet ﷺ without evidence.

But if by meditation we mean deep spiritual reflection, awareness of Allah, remembrance, contemplation of creation, Qur’an recitation, night prayer, supplication, and devotional retreat, then the life of the Prophet ﷺ provides clear and powerful examples.

Before prophethood, he worshipped in seclusion in Hira through taḥannuth.

After prophethood, he practiced contemplation through dhikr, Qur’an, tahajjud, reflection, supplication, and iʿtikāf.

His example teaches us that the heart should not be empty. It should be awake to Allah.

And reflection should not end in thought alone. It should lead to worship, repentance, gratitude, and better character.

May Allah grant us the ihsan described by the Prophet ﷺ: that we worship Him as though we see Him, and that when we cannot reach that level, we never forget that He sees us.

May Allah purify our hearts, make our private lives better than our public lives, grant us sincere worship, beneficial reflection, and truthful repentance.

May Allah bless our master Muhammad ﷺ, his family, his Companions, and all those who follow him with excellence.

Āmīn.
